Climate Archetype, Not Controller Choice, Drives Peak Demand Trajectories: Evidence for Portfolio-Level Resilience Under Climate Change Abstract Climate archetype—not controller choice—determines long-term peak demand trajectories under warming, yet controllers deliver substantial immediate savings. We demonstrate this through a controller-agnostic benchmark coupling EnergyPlus with four comfort-driven strategies (rule- based Reference, PMV-based, PINN-VAE, LightGBM) across ﬁve ASHRAE climate zones (2A–6A) and three time horizons (Historical 2001–2020, Mid-century 2041–2060, Late-century 2081–2100; RCP8.5). Variance decomposition reveals that climate archetype explains 90.6% of absolute peak variance and 87.9% of peak ampliﬁcation under warming, while controllers contribute only 5.0% to peak levels and 1.3% to growth rates—controllers reduce peaks proportionally across horizons, leaving climate- driven trajectories unchanged. Despite minimal inﬂuence on trajectories, controller choice delivers immediate economic value: predictive controllers achieve ”CP95 →17–41kW per 10,000 m2(tari!-window 95th-percentile demand reduction), equivalent to $510–$1,290/10,000 m2·month at $30/kW demand charges. These ﬁndings decouple two decisions: controller selection for immediate peak reduction versus climate-aware infrastruc- ture planning for long-term resilience. Introduction Does controller choice matter for long-term grid resilience under climate change, or only for today’s demand charges? This question has practical urgency: utilities and building operators must decide whether to invest in advanced controls expecting durable beneﬁts, or whether climate- driven load growth will overwhelm any controller advantage. We show that the answer depends on the outcome metric—controllers substantially reduce peak levels but do not alter peak trajectories , which are dominated by climate archetype. Building energy systems face dual pressures under climate change: maintaining occupant com- fort during intensifying heatwaves [1, 2] while managing grid stress from coincident cooling peaks [3, 4]. Controller choices shape both outcomes, yet energy savings (kWh) and peak reduction (kW) can decouple [5], and strategies optimized for present conditions may fail under future weather or in di!erent climates [6]. Grid-interactive e"cient building (GEB) frameworks emphasize demand re- sponse and peak shaving [7, 8], but typically treat comfort as a soft constraint without multi-metric validation or future-weather robustness [9, 10]. Research gaps. Two speciﬁc gaps motivate this work. First, controller benchmarks remain conﬁned to single sites [11] or present-day scenarios [12], precluding climate-generalized conclu- sions. Second, climate impact assessments quantify warming e!ects on building loads [13, 14] but rarely compare control strategies or report grid-facing metrics (demand-window peaks, ramps, load factors). Critically, no prior study tests whether scalar stress indices—combining temperature intensity, tari! structure, and building ﬂexibility—can predict peak ampliﬁcation across diverse climates. We address this gap directly and ﬁnd that such indices fail: a proposed stress metric !shows no signiﬁcant pooled association with peak ampliﬁcation ( p=0.203; signiﬁcant only in hot-humid climates after FDR correction).
