Title : A Longitudinal Review of Indoor CO₂ in Buildings: Interpretations , Applications , and Technologies Abstract: Key words: 2 1 Introduction In the early stages of indoor environmental monitoring, CO₂ was regarded as a fundamental indicator for assessing air quality. Consequently, early CO₂ sensing technologies emerged, and during their initial phase, they were considered highly practical as th ey satisfied preliminary monitoring requirements. However, in subsequent practical applications, these early sensors revealed critical underlying technical flaws. The most central issue was their extremely poor "settling time" —the sensors exhibited sluggis h responses to environmental changes, making it difficult to provide timely and accurate data. This severe technical deficiency led academia and the engineering community to label them as "less useful" during that specific historical period, thereby plungi ng them into prolonged decline and marginalization. In recent years, however, there are some more development that attempts to link indoor CO₂ concentrations to higher human cognitive benefits and a reduced risk of various disease transmission pathways. This entirely new paradigm of health awareness has directly triggered a new wave of monitoring demands, sparking a robust resurgence of this once -marginalized sensing technology. Given the entirely new dimensions of value demonstrated by contemporary CO₂ monitoring, existing literature rarely offers a systema tic retrospective of this winding evolutionary trajectory. Therefore, this study aims to take this opportunity to look back and conduct a profound analysis of what exactly transpired behind the overarching trend of the "rise, fall, and resurgence" of CO₂ sensing technologies. We aim to explore and answer the follo wing core questions: What technical architectures were employed when these sensors were initially developed? Why were they useful in the beginning, and upon what criteria was this utility based? What specific setbacks did they encounter in practical applic ations that led to them being deemed "less useful" due to flaws like settling time? And most importantly, given contemporary insights and demands, why is there an urgent desire to bring them back into the public eye and endow them with a new mission? 3 1.1 Previous related literature reviews The historical development of indoor CO 2 monitoring in the built environment reflects a dynamic interplay among engineering demand, technological maturation, and evolving conceptions of human well -being. A foundational synthesis by Emmerich and Persily (Emmerich & Persily, 52) in 2001 represents one of the first systematic efforts to consolidate the roles of CO 2 in indoor environmental control. While this seminal work was inextricably linked to the emergence of CO 2-based Demand Controlled Ventilation (DCV), it articulated the fundamental rational e for employing CO 2 as a tracer -gas indicator of overall ventilation performance. Grounded in mass -balance physics, the report outlined early applications of nondispersive infrared (NDIR) sensing and its implications for building energy operation. Nevertheless, this analysis was largely embedded within an “efficiency -oriented” paradigm, where CO 2 was primarily interpreted as an operational signal for energy conservation in response to late - twentieth -century energy volatility. Subsequent studies in the mid -2000s expanded the discourse toward the socio - technical, regulatory, and practical dimensions of sensor deployment. Apte (Apte, M. G, 53) examined market adoption and the "human factor" barriers influencing the widespread use of CO 2 monitoring, highlighting sensor reliability, calibration drift, and maintenance costs as critical technical constraints. In parallel, Murphy (Mahyuddin and Awbi , 54) shifted the research focus from theoretical feasibility toward operational realization. By addressing engineering implementation, commissioning practices, and compliance with standards like ASHRAE 62.1, these works established the practical infrastru cture for CO 2 monitoring that remains the baseline for contemporary building science. Despite their foundational importance, these early reviews now represent a historically bounded phase of research. Much of the existing literature remains fragmented, either siloed within sensor hardware physics or narrowly focused on ventilation control l ogic, without fully integrating how technological advances reshape the interpretation and use of CO₂ data across broader domains. Furthermore, many 4 earlier syntheses predate the emerging “information –health” paradigm of the 2020s, in which indoor CO₂ is increasingly examined not only as a ventilation indicator but also in relation to occupant health, performance, and exposure dynamics. Empirical and review studies have demonstrated associations between indoor CO₂, ventilation conditions, and health or learning outcomes in buildings (Fisk, 56), while controlled experiments have reported measurable declines in decision -making performance at moderately elevated CO₂ concentrations (Satish et al., 57). More recently, CO₂ has been reframed as a practical proxy for rebreathed air and airborne infection risk in shared indoor environments, particularly in the context of the COVID -19 pandemic (Peng & Jimenez, 58). Collectively, these developments signal a cognitive and epidemiological reinterpretation of indoor CO₂ that is largely absent from earlier revie w literature. Accordingly, there is a compelling need for an updated synthesis that traces the co-evolution of sensing technology, application domains, and interpretative paradigms along a unified temporal trajectory. Recent integrative reviews have begun to connect indoor CO₂ concentration, ventilation demand, and broader indoor environmental performance (Lu et al., 59), yet a comprehensive historical and conceptual consolidation remains lacking. The present review responds to this need by revisiting the longitudinal development of indoor CO₂ monitoring and situating contemporary practices within their broader technologi cal and epistemic context. By integrating advances in IoT -enabled sensing with the methodological shift toward data -driven inference and health -centered indoor environmental research, this work aims to provide a unified perspective on the evolving role of CO₂ in the built environment. Long before this, however, people had already realized th at air in enclosed and crowded spaces could transmit disease, raising the question of what constituted “bad air.” Seventeenth - and eighteenth -century physiological experiments by Mayow (Mayow 1901, [1]) and Lavoisier first identified “igneo -aerial particles” in the air as CO₂ and associated the sensation of stuffiness with its accumulation rather than oxygen depletion (Janssen 1999, 01) . This view dominated for nearly a century until Pettenkofer’s seminal work in 1858 (Pettenkofer 1858, 01) demonstrated that CO₂ itself was not harmful (Klauss AK 1970) and proposed 0.1% (approximately 1000 ppm) as the upper limit for acceptable indoor air . This benchmark, later known as the “Pettenkofer number,” became the basis for CO₂ guidelines and influenced ventilation standards for decades. Building on this insight, Pettenkofer and Saeltzer (1872) further formalized CO₂ as a surrogate indicator of vitiated air (Tredgold 1836 , [2]), linking it to human bioeffluent generation and perceived air quality. Through the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, studies further found that insufficient ventilation primarily caused discomfort, characterized by unpleasant 13 body odors and increased body temperature, rather than physiological harm from CO₂ itself (what we know and should know about ventilation) . Subsequent research by Lemberg and Yaglou in the 1930s demonstrated that the perception of human body odor could serve as a criterion for determining ventilation rates —typically 7.5 –10 L/s per person (15 –20 cfm) —corresponding to a moderate level of comfort (Persily 2015 , 06- 1). Thus, CO₂ was again recognized not as a contaminant but as a proxy for bioeffluent concentrat ion (body odor) , since both CO₂ and bioeffluents are emitted proportionally to metabolic activity . 3.2 CO 2 as ventilation indicator (1980s) and DCV variable (1990s) In the latter half of the twentieth century, CO₂ became increasingly recognized for its role in representing ventilation needs in buildings. ASHRAE Standard 62 -1981 (ASHRAE Standard 62 -1981) introduced the “Indoor Air Quality (IAQ) Procedure,” using indoor CO₂ levels as a proxy for occupant -generated pollutants. Pioneering experiments by Fanger (Fanger 1983, 01) , Cain (Cain 1983, 01) and other researchers established quantitative relationships between CO₂ concentration, ventilation rate, and perceived air f reshness. By 1989 , ASHRAE Standard 62 -1989 (ASHRAE Standard 62 - 1989) included a 1000 ppm guideline in Appendix D, presented as an example linking per-person outdoor airflow to steady -state indoor CO₂ levels. Although this value was never intended as a strict limit, it became broadly adopted as a practical rule of thumb for assessing whether a space was likely adequately ventilated for its occupancy. Around the same period, field studies (Smith 1988, 03; Sandberg 1985, 03 ) demonstrated that CO₂ could serve as a safe tracer gas for estimating air change rates, extending its use from comfort resea rch to ventilation diagnostics. In summary, the 1980s established CO₂ as a practical metric for ventilation efficacy, grounded in both human comfort science and ventilation engineering principles. During the 1990s, attention shifted toward actively using CO₂ measurements to evaluate and control ventilation in real time , marked by the development of DCV. Enabled by continuous CO₂ sensing, DCV systems adjusted outdoor air intake in 14 response to real occupancy, achieving significant energy savings while maintaining acceptable IAQ. A pivotal development came with the 1997 ASHRAE interpretation (IC 62 -1989 -27) (ASHRAE Interpretation 1997) , which formally endorsed CO₂ -based DCV under Standard 62 -1989. It outlined procedures for modulating outdoor air intake according to indoor CO₂ , thereby formally legitimizing CO₂ -based control for heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HV AC ) design. 3.3 CO 2 as limited IAQ proxy ( late 1990s -early 2000s ) During the late 1990s and early 2000s, CO₂ underwent a conceptual correction rather than expansion. The shift was preceded by clarifications in the early 1990s, when official standards began reevaluating the meaning of the widely cited 1000 ppm benchmark. An ASHRAE interpretation (IC 62 -1989 -7) (ASHRAE Interpretation 1993) clarified that 1000 ppm “does not necessarily constitute good air quality” and that CO₂ should not be viewed as a stand -alone contaminant metric. The document also addressed misconceptions that CO₂ control could replace the outdoor air requirements in Stan dard 62 -1989, emphasizing that filtration cannot substitute for ventilation except under defined conditions. This clarification helped dispel confusion, especially since occupational exposure limits such as OSHA’s 5000 ppm threshold [3] were based on safet y rather than comfort. By the late 1990s , a more critical and nuanced understanding of what CO₂ could — and could not —reveal about indoor environments began to emerge. Persily’s 1997 paper (Persily 1997, 01) synthesized best practices and clarified the limitations of CO₂ - based assessment. He emphasized that while indoor CO₂ can indicate whether bioeffluent odors are likely acceptable or help estimate air change rates under steady - state conditions, it “does no t provide a comprehensive indication of indoor air quality.” Persily cauti oned against oversimplified interpretations and highlighted key considerations such as system configuration, temporal variation in occupancy, additional CO₂ sources (e.g., gas appliances), sensor placement , and calibration. His guidance helped prevent over -reliance on CO₂ as a sole measure of IAQ. In essence, 15 the 1990s established CO₂ -based occupancy control as a mainstream ventilation strategy while also fostering a more nuanced understanding that CO₂ represents only one aspect of indoor air quality. By the end of the decade, ASHRAE had removed any fixed indoor CO₂ “limit” from its standards (Persily 2020, 06 -1)—since 1989, no ASHRAE standard has included a numeric CO₂ criterion for acceptability to avoid misinterpretation . A parallel reinterpretation occurred in research on sick building syndrome (SBS). SBS refers to a set of non -specific symptoms experienced by occupants of a building, where the symptoms increase with time spent in the building and improve upon leaving, without an identifiable medical cause. Early epidemiological studies —particularly analyses of the U.S. EPA BASE office -building dataset in the late 1990s and early 2000s (Shaw 1998; Kemp 1998, 07 -1)—reported positive associations between indoor – outdoor CO₂ differentials and the prevalence of SBS symptoms among office workers. For instance, Seppänen et al. (1999 , 07-1) reviewed 21 studies involving more than 30,000 participants and concluded that elevated indoor CO₂ concentrations were frequently associated with increased SBS symptoms, although t hey noted measurement differences and methodological limitations across studies. Subsequent work emphasized, however, that CO₂ likely served as a proxy for occupancy -related pollutants and ventilation rate rather than a direct causal agent (Apte et al. 2000 , 07-1). These findings contributed to a shift in terminology from SBS to “building -related symptoms” and “building -related illness ,” and reinforced the conceptualization of CO₂ as an indicator of broader indoor air quality rather than a primary toxicant (de 2010, 04). 3.4 CO 2 as control indicator (2000s) In the 2000s, the role of indoor CO₂ shifted from an experimental control variable to a standardized operational indicator embedded in building regulations and routine design practice. Revisions of ASHRAE Standard 62.1 (2001, 2004, 2007) formalized demand -controlled ventilation (DCV), including occupancy -based dynamic reset of 16 outdoor air intake. Consequently, CO₂ -based control moved from a novel strategy to a common requirement in commercial buildings and energy codes (Apte 2006, 02) , and sensors became typical components of HV AC systems (Fisk 2010, 02) . Concurrently , CO₂ monitoring expanded beyond mechanical systems into mixed -mode and naturally ventilated buildings [5]. Here, CO₂ functioned as real -time feedback linking architectural capacity (windows, shafts, layout) with mechanical assistance, triggering ventilation when natural airflow proved insufficient. This integration fostered closer collaboration between archit ectural design and system operation. Research during this period focused on ensuring reliable application rather than proving feasibility, addressing multi -zone control, sensor placement, response characteristics, and occupant generation rates, while standardized measurement procedures were i ntroduced (ASTM E741) . By the end of the decade, the consensus in standards framed indoor CO₂ should be used as an operational indicator and control input, not a health limit. Overall, the 2000s established CO₂ as a routine control indicator governing ventilation performance across both mechanical and hybrid building systems. 3.5 CO 2 as exposure proxy (2010s) and infection -risk proxy ( 2020 s-present ) In the 2010s, indoor CO₂ expanded beyond a ventilation control indicator and began to be interpreted as an exposure proxy linked to occupant well -being. Laboratory and field studies examined whether moderate CO₂ concentrations could directly affect cogniti ve performance and health (Jaber 2017, 07 -2), producing mixed evidence but shifting attention toward occupant outcomes rather than ventilation alone. Regardless of causality, lower CO₂ levels increasingly became associated with improved perceived air quali ty and productivity because they indicated reduced accumulation of rebreathed air. During the same period, CO₂ has been incorporated into numerous standards and rating systems as a readily measurable surrogate for IAQ . International guidelines adopted concentration thresholds as operational benchmarks: EN 13779:2007 classified IAQ categories using indoor –outdoor CO₂ differentials , the UK’s Building Bulletin 101 17 (2018) specified limits for classrooms, and green building certifications such as WELL promoted continuous monitoring around ~800 ppm . These frameworks positioned CO₂ as a contextual indicator of shared -air exposure rather than a strict pollutant limit. By the 2020s, the interpretation of indoor CO₂ further shifted toward infection - risk assessment. Studies during the COVID -19 pandemic demonstrated that CO₂ concentration covaries with rebreathed air fraction and therefore potential airborne pathogen exposu re (Peng and Jimenez 2021, 08) . As a result, CO₂ monitoring was adopted in classrooms, restaurants, and public buildings as a practical risk -management tool. Contemporary standards now frame CO₂ as a contextual performance indicator: elevated concentration s reliably signal inadequate ventilation, whereas low CO₂ alone does not ensure good IAQ, which depends on multiple factors beyond CO₂ itself (ASHRAE position document) . In conclusion, CO₂ has evolved from a nineteenth -century vitiation indicator to a twenty -first-century diagnostic and control metric for ventilation performance, as summarized in Figure 7. This longitudinal development reflects a broader shift in architectural and environmental thinking —from perceiving air as invisible backdrop to managing it as a measurable, controllable, and designable environmental system.
